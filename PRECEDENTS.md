# PRECEDENTS — every design decision beside who else does it

**Why each choice in dromas is the usual one, or where it is not, and why.** Part 1 sets dromas beside whole systems. Part 2 sets each decision beside the established systems and published standards that make it, with their numbers, and says where dromas differs and why. Part 3 does the same for the reference server's own build choices, which are no part of the protocol. `§` is a section of `PROTOCOL.md`.

**Rules for this file:**
- **A row is added the day a big decision on the wire or in the security model is made,** with the decision. A client's own behaviour (its command line, its files, its lock) carries its precedent beside the code that does it.
- **A row holds the decision in a line with its section, each precedent with its numbers and its source, and where dromas differs and why.** Nothing else: not how the decision was reached, not the spec restated, not what a standard is, which its reader knows.
- **Every claim about another system says how it is known**, in one of three ways: `read <date>`, with the source, when it was read at its source that day · `review 2026-10-10`, when it comes from the design reviews of that day and the sources they cited (§ Sources), and has not been read again since · `from memory`, when it has not been looked up. A number is never quoted from memory as if it were read. A quotation is its source's words, a shorter one a part of them, and is kept only where the words are the norm. If a row is wrong, an issue is welcome.
- **A precedent is industry practice:** a major public API, a published standard, a widely deployed protocol.
- **This is the one place a comparison is kept.** `PROTOCOL.md` carries none, and points here.

# Part 1 — dromas beside whole systems

## 1a. Secure messaging systems

**In one line:** dromas is roughly Signal without the ratchets or sealed sender, plus self-certifying addresses and signatures that can be shown to others: a modern, post-quantum, verified-sender PGP. *(review 2026-10-10.)*

| System | Identity and key distribution | Encryption | Forward secrecy / post-compromise / post-quantum | Sender authentication | Hidden from the server |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Signal** | Phone number or username + identity key, from Signal's directory; safety numbers out of band | PQXDH, then the Double Ratchet; since October 2025 SPQR adds an ML-KEM-768 ratchet | yes / yes / yes | Implicit in the ratchet; deniable | Contents; the sender (sealed sender) |
| **WhatsApp** | Signal-style, plus key transparency (AKD, 2023) | Signal protocol | yes / yes / not documented | Implicit; deniable | Contents |
| **iMessage** | Device keys signed by a Contact Key Verification account key (2023) | PQ3 (2024): Kyber-1024, then a Kyber-768 ratchet | yes / yes / yes | Every message signed by a Secure Enclave P-256 device key: the closest deployed analogue of dromas's signed prekey plus a signature on each message | Contents |
| **Proton Mail** | Email address; keys served by Proton, private keys stored there under the user's password | OpenPGP | no / no / opt-in since 2026-05-05 | OpenPGP signatures, which can be shown to others | Body and attachments; NOT subject or addresses |
| **MLS** (RFC 9420) | Credentials sign one-time KeyPackages, but for a "last resort" one: dromas's reusable prekey | TreeKEM groups | yes / yes / by suite | Signatures inside the group | Contents; the sender within the group |
| **SimpleX** | No user identifiers: one-way queues whose addresses are capabilities, exchanged out of band | Double Ratchet + sntrup761 (default for direct chats since v5.7, 2024) + a NaCl layer per queue | yes / yes / yes | Implicit; deniable | Contents, sender, recipient, and their link |
| **Nostr DMs** (NIP-17) | A secp256k1 key is the identity | NIP-44, with a signed "seal" inside an ephemeral-key "gift wrap" (NIP-59) | no / no / no | A signature INSIDE the encryption | Contents; the sender |
| **Noise one-way X** (rev 34, 2018; 1b) | None: accepting a key is "up to the application" (§14) | `e, es, s, ss`, then AEAD | sender compromise only (§7.7) / no / no | Static-static DH: recipient-only, open to key-compromise impersonation, deniable, replayable | Contents; the sender's static key (§7.8) |
| **dromas** | A self-certifying address (the identity's hash) + a prekey the identity signs, or a working key it certified signs: no directory to trust | age; ML-KEM-768 + X25519 | bounded by how often a mailbox checks in (about two days for one read daily) / no / yes | Ed25519 by the identity, or a working key it certified, inside the encryption: checked by the recipient, unseen by the server, names its recipient, can be shown to others | Contents only, by design |

**Where dromas stands**

| Dimension | Verdict | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Getting the right key | **Ahead** of directory-based messengers; **level** with key-as-identity systems | Signal, WhatsApp, iMessage and Proton serve keys from their own directory and add safety numbers or key transparency against substitution. A dromas address IS the fingerprint, as in Nostr, did:key, Tor onion addresses and SimpleX's links. What dromas adds is that rotation and a change of key type never move the address |
| Content encryption | **On par** with Signal and iMessage; **ahead** of Proton | A post-quantum hybrid, through a reviewed format |
| Sender authentication | **Stronger for audit; not deniable** | Provable to others, and the server holds no signature (Part 2, § 3). iMessage also signs; Signal, WhatsApp, SimpleX and Noise's one-way patterns are deniable. Right for bots acting on messages; a privacy cost elsewhere |
| Forward secrecy, post-compromise security | **Behind** the ratcheting messengers; **level** with every non-interactive scheme; **ahead** of Proton and PGP | Per-message forward secrecy needs interaction, which no non-interactive scheme has (Noise one-way, HPKE, PGP); no self-healing, which is too much state for stateless bot messages |
| Metadata privacy | **Behind** Signal, SimpleX and Nostr; **ahead** of Proton on subjects | The server sees who sends, at the send, since verified senders are the product, and keeps no sender once a message is stored (Part 2, § 3); the subject is inside the envelope |
| Operator trust | **Ahead** of Proton; roughly Signal-like | The server never holds a private key in any form; Proton stores them server-side behind a password |

## 1b. In Noise's terms

*(review 2026-10-10, against Noise rev 34 §7.4, §7.7, §7.8, §13 and §14, and PQNoise.)*

- **dromas is N made post-quantum** (age's HPKE with the X-Wing KEM) **plus an outer Ed25519 signature:** sender authentication others can check and that resists key-compromise impersonation, which no one-way pattern reaches (source property 2); destination property 2, replayable, which the recipient's seen-set handles; forward secrecy against a recipient's compromise, bounded by rotation.
- **X and K are not for stored bot messages:** a leaked prekey would let anyone forge any sender to that mailbox; they are deniable; rev 34 has no post-quantum, and PQNoise shows an authenticated one-way K or X cannot be built from KEMs alone, so a post-quantum design needs a signature anyway; no stock command-line tool; and §14 keeps a Noise static key inside Noise.
- **The one deployed store-and-forward use found,** Bitchat's courier path (Noise X), states it has no forward secrecy.
- **Taken from Noise:** §13's padding, after the signature; payloads that ignore what they do not know; the vocabulary of security properties.

# Part 2 — each decision beside its precedent

## 1. How a mailbox proves itself to the server: a pass it signs for itself

**Ours** (§8): the mailbox's signer, its identity or a working key it certified, signs a four-line pass (tag, address, server name, end), sent in `Authorization` on every call; a client asks 10 minutes, a server takes up to an hour, and issues and keeps nothing.

| Who | What they do | Their numbers | How it is known |
|---|---|---|---|
| **GitHub Apps** | The app signs a JWT with its own key, sent as `Bearer` | `exp` at most 10 minutes ahead; `iat` recommended 60 seconds back, "to protect against clock drift" | read 2026-10-12: [GitHub Docs, Generating a JWT for a GitHub App](https://docs.github.com/en/apps/creating-github-apps/authenticating-with-a-github-app/generating-a-json-web-token-jwt-for-a-github-app) |
| **Google service accounts** | The application signs a JWT with the account's key and sends it straight to the API, no authorization server | an hour | read 2026-10-12: [Google, Using OAuth 2.0 for Server to Server Applications, Addendum: Service account authorization without OAuth](https://developers.google.com/identity/protocols/oauth2/service-account) |
| **Web Push, VAPID** (RFC 8292) | The application server signs a JWT, sent as `Authorization: vapid t=…, k=…`, with the push service's origin as `aud` | `exp` "MUST NOT be more than 24 hours from the time of the request" | read 2026-10-12: [RFC 8292](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8292) |
| **Apple Push Notification service** | The provider signs a token with its own key | an hour; refreshed every 20 to 60 minutes | the hour: [the archived guide](https://developer.apple.com/library/archive/documentation/NetworkingInternet/Conceptual/RemoteNotificationsPG/CommunicatingwithAPNs.html); the 20 and 60: Apple's wording as quoted on [its forums](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/687789), search results read 2026-10-12, Apple's current page not loaded; algorithm and header from memory |

**Where ours sits:** 10 minutes asked is GitHub's ceiling, the shortest; an hour taken is Google's and Apple's. The gap does the job of GitHub's 60 seconds of backdating, from the other end (§ 3a). The server's name is VAPID's `aud`, and a scheme of its own with named parameters is VAPID's shape.

**Where ours differs, and why:**
- **SSHSIG, not a JWT:** stock `ssh-keygen` makes and checks a pass, and § 2's certificate, where a working key signs, travels inside the signature.
- **The key is the address's, not registered:** the four hold the caller's key on file; a pass is the identity's, or chains to it, and the address is its hash.
- **A server reads the header as RFC 9110 §11 lets a recipient, but refuses two things a VAPID or RFC 9110 reader would take:** an unknown parameter, where VAPID's "MUST be ignored" (RFC 8292 §3), since an addition here is a new scheme (§14); and a quoted-pair, which RFC 9110 has a recipient unescape, where no value of ours needs one and a sender "SHOULD NOT generate a quoted-pair in a quoted-string except where necessary to quote DQUOTE and backslash octets" (§5.6.4). Go has no auth-param reader (`net/http` reads only `Basic`; `httpguts` "comes with no API stability promise"), so `ParsePass` is ours, held by `testdata/vectors/pass/` and `FuzzParsePass`. *Read 2026-10-20: RFC 9110, RFC 8292, Go 1.27.2's `net/http/request.go`, `httpguts` at x/net v0.60.0.*

**Considered and not chosen:** a server-issued bearer token (RFC 6750), which needs issuing, storing and rotating and lasts until replaced when it leaks *(review 2026-10-10)*; a signature over each request, as AWS Signature Version 4, HTTP Message Signatures (RFC 9421) and DPoP (RFC 9449) *(from memory)*, which for a send would bind the recipient and the ciphertext and hand the server a proof of who sent what to whom (§ 3).

## 2. The identity signs, or certifies a working key that signs: both are taken

**Ours** (§5): the address is the identity's hash. The identity signs its mailbox's prekey statements, passes and messages itself, or a working key it certified in an OpenSSH certificate signs them in its place. A verifier takes both: the identity's signature always, and a certificate above the revocation of certificates it holds, which never covers the identity (§10.2). A certificate runs as long as its holder chooses, up to its server's maximum, and is judged at the verifier's clock with no ceiling there.

**The identity signs what the mailbox says:**

| Who | What they do | How it is known |
|---|---|---|
| **Signal** | The long-term identity key signs the signed prekey itself: a bundle carries "Sig(IK<sub>B</sub>, Encode(SPK<sub>B</sub>))" | read 2026-10-31, [The X3DH Key Agreement Protocol](https://signal.org/docs/specifications/x3dh/) §2.4, §3.2 |
| **WhatsApp** | Signal's scheme: the identity key signs the signed prekey | from memory |
| **GitHub Apps, Google service accounts, Web Push VAPID, APNs** | The caller's own long-lived key signs each short-lived token, with no key between | § 1 |
| **iMessage** | A device key signs every message | review 2026-10-10 (§ 3) |

**A primary key delegates:**

| Who | What they do | How it is known |
|---|---|---|
| **OpenSSH certificates** | A CA key certifies a key with principals, a serial and validity; dromas uses the format unchanged, made by `ssh-keygen -s` | `draft-ietf-sshm-cert` (from `draft-miller-ssh-cert`, which replaced `PROTOCOL.certkeys` in 2025); measured on OpenSSH 8.1p1 and 10.2p1, 2026-10-12 |
| **OpenPGP** | A primary key certifies signing and encryption subkeys, so it can stay offline; signatures by the primary key and by a signing subkey are both taken, the "sign data" key flag being a flag of a key, primary or subkey, and a subkey binding names a signing subkey; a revocation of a subkey is of that subkey: "A revoked subkey is not to be used" | read 2026-10-31, [RFC 9580](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9580) §5.2.1.8, §5.2.1.12, §5.2.3.29; that it can stay offline, from memory |
| **Tor relays** | The Ed25519 master identity key signs only a medium-term signing key, so that it can be kept offline | read 2026-10-31, [Tor spec, relay keys](https://spec.torproject.org/tor-spec/relay-keys.html) |
| **did:plc** | Rotation keys sign the identity's operations; a separate signing key signs its content | from memory |
| **Matrix cross-signing** | A master key signs the device-signing and user-signing keys | from memory |
| **MLS** | A credential signs short-lived KeyPackages: dromas's prekey, one level down | review 2026-10-10 |

**How long a delegated key lasts:**

| Who | What they do | How it is known |
|---|---|---|
| **Let's Encrypt** | Halves its certificates' lifetime: 90 days "will be cut in half to 45 days by 2028", with a 64-day default from 2027-02-10, following the CA/Browser Forum's Baseline Requirements; it recommends renewing "at approximately two thirds of the way through the current certificate’s lifetime" | read 2026-10-31, [Decreasing Certificate Lifetimes to 45 Days](https://letsencrypt.org/2025/12/02/from-90-to-45) and [64-Day Certificate Lifetimes Coming Feb 2027](https://letsencrypt.org/2026/10/07/64-day-certs) |
| **Short-lived certificates in place of revocation** | The web waives revocation at 10 days or less, 7 from 2026-03-15 (§ 3b) | read 2026-10-13 |

**Where ours differs, and why:**
- **Both forms taken, as OpenPGP takes the primary key's signatures and its signing subkeys':** each other system above fixes one. A mailbox that never keeps its identity away from daily work has nothing to certify, as Signal's identity signs its prekey; one that does signs under a working key, and a revocation of certificates ends working keys alone, as OpenPGP's of a subkey leaves the primary key. Refusing the identity's own signature would stop no thief: whoever holds the identity certifies a key of its own as easily (§15.5).
- **Statements go one way by their order:** the identity ranks below every certificate (§5.3), so a statement under a certificate retires the identity's statements at a server and at a sender, as a later certificate retires an earlier one's. Messages and passes are not ordered.
- **Not NIP-26's delegation,** where a Nostr key names a delegate in a tag any event can carry and nothing revokes the delegation (from memory): here a certificate is revoked through its serial (§10.2).
- **The lifetime is the holder's, bounded at the server alone:** a server publishes its maximum (§11.18), as a CA sets its own, and a reader judges only the window, so a server that lowers its maximum strands no message already signed. The reference server's default follows Let's Encrypt's 45 days, with the hour of room (§ 3a).
- **Validity at the verifier's clock,** as X.509 without a trusted timestamp, where OpenPGP takes the signer's stated time (both from memory): nobody here can vouch for when anything was signed.
- **A newer certificate does not retire an older one for messages already signed;** it does at the server and for prekey statements.

## 2a. A certificate's serial is taken from its maker's clock

**Ours** (§5): the serial is the maker's clock in seconds, or one above the highest the mailbox knows when the clock gives nothing higher; a verifier compares serials and never reads one as a time. A mailbox restored from its identity alone thereby numbers above every certificate made before.

| Who | What they do | How it is known |
|---|---|---|
| **X.509** | A serial "MUST be unique for each certificate issued by a given CA", kept so by the issuer's records; nothing orders two | RFC 5280 §4.1.2.2, read 2026-10-14 |
| **The web's certificate authorities** | 64 or more random bits: unique without a count | from memory |
| **OpenSSH certificates** | The CA numbers as it chooses; a KRL revokes "by serial number or key ID" | `PROTOCOL.certkeys` at `V_9_9_P1` and `PROTOCOL.krl` on `master`, read 2026-10-14 |
| **BIND 9, a zone's serial** | `serial-update-method unixtime`: seconds since the epoch, "unless the serial number is already greater than or equal to that value, in which case it is simply incremented by one." The rule here | `doc/arm/reference.rst` on `main`, read 2026-10-14; that secondaries only compare: from memory |
| **PowerDNS, signing as it answers** | `SOA-EDIT` `EPOCH` sets the serial to the epoch seconds, for our reason: a live signer keeps no count to raise | `docs/dnssec/operational.rst` on `master`, read 2026-10-14 |
| **RFC 1912** | Recommends `YYYYMMDDnn` | §2.2, read 2026-10-14 |
| **WireGuard's handshake** | An encrypted TAI64N timestamp; the receiver keeps the greatest per client and drops anything not above it: a sender's clock held to an order by a receiver that only compares | wireguard.com/protocol, read 2026-10-14 |

**Where ours differs, and why:**
- **An identity is a key, not an issuer with records:** a mailbox is restored from its key alone (§12.4), and the only record it could consult is its untrusted server's, which never saw a certificate made and not published.
- **The serial must order as well as be unique** (§5): a random one gives no order, and a clock is what a key with no records has that rises.
- **The serial carries the order, not `valid after`,** which every verifier reads as a time and so cannot go one above the last when a clock reads behind; and revocation runs through a serial, as a KRL's range does.
- **It rests on the identity holder's clock;** the spec names where that gives way (a clock that read ahead, a behind-clock restore against an older record, two machines in one second).

## 3. The server holds no signature that proves who sent what to whom

**Ours** (§9.2, §13): sign, then encrypt: inside the ciphertext, the envelope's line, the signature block as `ssh-keygen` wrote it, then padding. The server knows who sends to whom at the send, from the pass its limits need, keeps no sender beside its recipient once a message is stored, and holds nothing signed that says so.

**The order: sign, then encrypt, with the signature inside.**

| Who | What they do | How it is known |
|---|---|---|
| **JOSE** | "normally producers should sign the message and then encrypt the result (thus encrypting the signature)": a Nested JWT | RFC 7519 §11.2 and §2, read 2026-10-13 |
| **OpenPGP** | A signed message inside the encrypted one | RFC 9580 §10.3, read 2026-10-13 |
| **S/MIME** | Either order: signing first hides the signers; enveloping first exposes them and lets the signature be checked without decrypting | RFC 8551 §3.7, read 2026-10-13 |
| **Nostr NIP-17** | A signed seal inside an ephemeral-key gift wrap | review 2026-10-10 |
| **Signal, WhatsApp** | Shared-key authentication: no signature exists to show | review 2026-10-10 (deniable); the mechanism from memory |
| **iMessage** | A device key signs every message, where its server sees it | review 2026-10-10 |
| **age's author** | Names the usual advice (age plus signify or OpenSSH) and each order's flaw; recommends no layout | words.filippo.io/dispatches/age-authentication, read 2026-10-13 |

**The layout: the signed bytes as they are, then the signature.**

| Who | How the signed bytes and the signature travel together | How it is known |
|---|---|---|
| **git** | A detached signature, SSH's among them, appended to a tag or in a commit's header; the payload not encoded | `gitformat-signature`, read 2026-10-13 |
| **OpenPGP, CMS** | Binary packets: the signed bytes as they are, beside the signature | RFC 9580 §10.3, read 2026-10-13; CMS from memory |
| **JWS, DSSE** | One JSON object, payload and signature as base64 members | RFC 7515 §7.2.1 and `secure-systems-lab/dsse` `envelope.md`, read 2026-10-13 |
| **JOSE, later** | An unencoded payload, where encoding is "unnecessary and/or an impediment to adoption" | RFC 7797, abstract, read 2026-10-13 |

All agree on the method, not the bytes. Ours is git's signed tag: the same signature format, a fixed overhead; JSON with base64 members makes the envelope a third larger in every ciphertext.

**What went wrong with that layout elsewhere, and the rule each gives a reader** (§9.2):

| What happened | The rule | How it is known |
|---|---|---|
| **gitsign** verified a re-encoded object, not its raw bytes; with a header given twice, git and gitsign read different trees | Verify the envelope's line exactly as decrypted, read members from those bytes, refuse a key given twice | GHSA-7rmh-48mx-2vwc (2026-05-08), read 2026-10-13 |
| **GitLab** took the signature header as a commit's last and counted what followed as signed | Each part has one place, nothing is searched for, nothing after the first END line is read | GitLab issue 407252 (2023-04-02), read 2026-10-13 |
| **git before 2.20** took two OpenPGP signatures, reading status lines and dropping the exit status | One signature, one identity, the verifier's exit status | M. Górny, "Attack on git signature verification via crafting multiple signatures" (2019-01-26), read 2026-10-13 |

None was a flaw in the layout: each was a verifier that read an object differently from its signer.

**Where ours differs, and why:**
- **The recipient is named in what is signed** (the namespace, the envelope's `to`), which git needs not: it stops a recipient passing a signed message on, the flaw age's author names for this order.
- **The sender's identity document rides in the envelope,** as a JWS may carry its key (RFC 7515 §4.1.3, from memory): a recipient checks a sender it has never met without asking the server.
- **A recipient decrypts before it verifies,** as every system above that signs inside; what is lost is S/MIME's signature checkable without the decryption key.
- **No sender is kept at rest, and none is listed** (§11.11, §13, §15.8): the signature inside attributes a message, as Signal's service keeps no record of who messages whom (its published answers to subpoenas, from memory), but with no sealed sender, since a send's pass is what the server's limits and the mailbox's ACL count. So nothing that would keep the pair is kept: the dedupe lasts while the message is live (§11.10 step 6), and one sender filling a mailbox is answered by its ACL, with no cap per pair.

## 3a. Clocks: every check is exact, and whatever is made leaves an hour of room

**Ours** (§3.4): every check is exact at the checker's clock; the maker of a time-limited thing leaves an hour. A certificate starts an hour back, a server takes a prekey up to an hour past 33 days and a pass up to 60 minutes against the 10 asked, and a reader takes a `sent_at` up to an hour ahead; a server takes a mailbox from under 10 minutes slow to 50 fast.

**The maker dates the start back, and the verifier's check is exact:**

| Who | What they do | Their numbers | How it is known |
|---|---|---|---|
| **X.509 path validation** (RFC 5280 §6.1.3) | Exact at the verifier | none | read 2026-10-13: [RFC 5280](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc5280) |
| **OpenSSH `ssh-keygen`** | Backdates by default; `sshd` exact | 59 seconds, down to the minute | read 2026-10-13: `ssh-keygen.c` at `V_10_2_P1` |
| **Let's Encrypt** | Backdates `notBefore` | 58 minutes 30 seconds measured; 0.9 of the profile's largest backdate | measured 2026-10-13 with `openssl s_client -connect letsencrypt.org:443`; Boulder's `issuance/cert.go` |
| **CA/Browser Forum Baseline Requirements** | Permit backdating | up to 48 hours | read 2026-10-13: [`docs/BR.md`](https://github.com/cabforum/servercert/blob/main/docs/BR.md) |
| **HashiCorp Vault**, PKI and SSH | Backdates | 30 seconds (`not_before_duration`) | read 2026-10-13: [Vault API, PKI](https://developer.hashicorp.com/vault/api-docs/secret/pki) and [SSH](https://developer.hashicorp.com/vault/api-docs/secret/ssh) |
| **Kubernetes' certificate signer** | Backdates | 5 minutes | read 2026-10-13: `pkg/controller/certificates/signer/signer.go` |

| **BIND `dnssec-signzone`** | Backdates a signature | an hour, "to allow for clock skew" | read 2026-10-13: its manual page, `-s` |
| **MLS** (RFC 9420 §7.2) | Expects synced clocks | clients "SHOULD maintain time synchronization" | read 2026-10-13: [RFC 9420](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9420) |

**The verifier of a short-lived token gives leeway:**

| Who | What they do | Their numbers | How it is known |
|---|---|---|---|
| **JWT** (RFC 7519 §4.1.4, §4.1.5) | Optional leeway | "usually no more than a few minutes" | read 2026-10-13: [RFC 7519](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc7519) |

**Where ours sits:** exact checks with the room in what is made are X.509's and OpenSSH's shape, and nobody above checks exactly at both ends. An hour is what Let's Encrypt, OpenMLS and BIND backdate, each making things strangers verify; 30 seconds to 5 minutes (Vault, Kubernetes, OpenSSH) is for one operator's machines, and dromas's mailboxes are strangers to each other. The pass's 10 minutes slow is twice Kerberos's 5; a `sent_at` an hour ahead is Mastodon's margin.

**Where ours differs, and why:**
- **No leeway at any verifier,** where JWT and Kerberos put theirs: every check is one comparison with no number in it to disagree on.
- **A refusal for a time is final, kept as refused and acked,** where SMTP would call it transient (a 4yz is for what "can be successful if repeated without any change", RFC 5321 §4.2.1, queued and retried, §4.5.4.1; greylisting delays on purpose, RFC 6647): a client with right clocks makes no such message (§15.7), so what is left is a wrong clock or a hand-made certificate, and dromas does not try to deliver past a party that misbehaves: the reader keeps its findings for its owner to judge (§12.3). Read 2026-10-19: [RFC 5321](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc5321), [RFC 6647](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc6647).

**Not read:** smallstep's default backdate.

## 3b. Revocation: the identity signs it, every client checks it, and nothing less revokes

**Ours** (§10.1, §10.2, §12.5): the identity signs a fixed statement with no date, reason or number; the record is that signature with the identity document, so it proves itself, and a client takes "revoked" only on a record that verifies, keeps it, and applies it to everything the address signed. Certificates are revoked the same way through one serial, everything up to it, the highest signed standing. Every server keeps a record of any address; a client asks and publishes at its own server.

**Where ours follows them:**
- **A mailbox that holds its own revocation signs nothing more,** nor a message by a signer it holds revoked (§12.5, §10.2), as GnuPG uses no key it holds revoked, whatever any server saw; the server refuses a revoked address every call, as ACME a deactivated account.
- **Revocation is the one, final way out of a server,** as ACME's deactivation: there is no delete of a mailbox, since a pass is a credential of minutes and an end it could make would let a leaked pass end an address. A mailbox pauses with its ACL (§11.14) and is forgotten when it stops renewing (§13).
- **Checks run against what the mailbox holds, with no call,** as OpenSSH's file and CRLite's set; what a client verified it keeps (§12.5).
- **A client asks again when its answer says nothing about now:** a record stands for good, and "none held" is signed by nobody, OCSP's answer with no `nextUpdate`, so a client asks before every post and for each sender in a drain (§12.5).
- **The key revokes itself, and the record travels through untrusted servers,** as an OpenPGP key's and a CRL: a server can withhold one and never make one, so X.509's delegate question does not arise.
- **A revocation is the identity's, wherever used,** as an OpenPGP key's on any keyserver; nothing in an address names a server (§4.2), so every server keeps any address's record, filled by upload and trusted for nothing, as X.509's repository (§11.15).
- **A lookup is for an address the asker holds, and nothing is listed:** keys.openpgp.org's rule and reason, since an address lets anyone write to a mailbox (§15.9); so servers are filled by upload, never by copying each other, where CRLite gathers everything.
- **A certificate is revocable** (§10.2): its server's maximum (§ 2) is past the web's 10-day waiver. It covers certificates alone, as OpenPGP's revocation of a subkey covers that subkey (§ 2). By serial, the time its certificate was made (§5): everything made before, on any machine whose clock did not read ahead of the revoking one's, as AWS, Entra ID and Firebase revoke what was issued before a point and a KRL's range from 1; the highest one held is all a verifier needs, and nothing is suspended.
- **A revoked address stays revoked:** nothing above undoes one but a hold, and there is none here.
- **No cache may keep an API answer,** since a `410` is cacheable by default.
- **The proof travels with the refusal,** as TLS staples: every `410 revoked` carries the record in `record`, an extension member RFC 9457 §3.2 allows (read 2026-10-22).

**Where ours differs, and why:**
- **A revoked address is `410` with its record,** where ACME answers `401` or `400 alreadyRevoked` bare: ACME's server is the authority, and here none is, so the caller is shown the proof. A `401` here is a pass a mailbox cures by signing another; nothing cures a revocation.
- **No reason and no date,** where OpenPGP and X.509 carry both: a fixed text can be signed years early and rebuilt by its verifier from the address, so nothing is parsed before it is trusted, and a date would be the signer's unprovable word. So there is one kind, OpenPGP's compromised. A mailbox only retired stops renewing and is forgotten (§13).
- **No slot for a later field,** where OpenPGP, X.509 and KRLs have one with a critical flag: refusing what it cannot read makes a reader treat a revoked address as live, and without the flag a field is only a hint, the unused slot RFC 9170 says fails. A later addition is a second statement under a new tag, as GnuPG says to make a new revocation with a reason.
- **Three lines signed with SSHSIG, not a KRL,** OpenSSH's own format for this: Go reads no KRL and declined to, so each client would write a binary reader to say one number; SSHSIG is what `ssh-keygen(1)` recommends for a KRL that travels.
- **No list, no version, no way to revoke fewer,** where CRLs and KRLs are rising-numbered lists that a later one can shrink, giving two holders two verdicts; one serial cannot cover less than a lower one. The cost, sparing an earlier certificate while revoking a later, nothing here needs: a mailbox holds one certificate at its server, and its next is numbered above every one revoked (§5).
- **No date on a revoked certificate,** for the same reason: everything it signed is lost, its owner's unread messages too, which are sent again.
- **Nothing is ever dropped from it,** where a CRL drops expired entries: one serial is fixed in size, replaced by a higher, kept for good.
- **Neither client writes a revocation unasked,** where GnuPG and Sequoia write one beside every key. Theirs covers a forgotten passphrase (our reading: neither says why); a dromas identity has none, so a copy beside it is lost and stolen with it, and a liability once the identity moves elsewhere. A passphrase is planned (#50), and with it their default applies. GnuPG's import guard is here the address written in full.
- **A revocation of certificates lower than the one held is `409 conflict`, with the one held named,** where ACME answers a certificate already revoked `400 alreadyRevoked` (RFC 8555 §7.6, read 2026-10-22): RFC 9110 suggests `409` for a `PUT` inconsistent with the target's state (§9.3.4, read 2026-10-31), and the caller needs the higher record, which it reads and keeps.
- **No answer stops a drain and holds a send; "none held" does neither:** neither of the web's two. A browser could not afford to refuse without an answer; a mailbox can, since the server it waits on is the one its messages pass through. It is OpenSSH's rule for an unreadable file.
- **One source, the mailbox's own server,** where Sequoia lists five keyservers and GnuPG ships none. A second server guards only against one withholding a record it holds, and only if the revoked mailbox's owner and its reader chose the same one; none could be a default, since a lookup tells it whom its asker writes to. The mailbox's server already fails closed, as OpenSSH's `RevokedKeys` and OpenSSL's `-crl_check` do (RFC 5280 leaves it to the relying party). What remains is §15.6, a hostile server withholding a revocation of a mailbox it hosts; the norm against it is key transparency (WhatsApp's, Apple's, CONIKS, from memory), which this protocol does not have.
- **What costs a server is counted against its caller's address, and a flood of what it answers from memory on one budget** (§11.5), as keys.openpgp.org limits lookups and Let's Encrypt's per-endpoint limits run 5 to 1,000 a second (Part 2, § 4, Limits). Records kept for addresses with no mailbox are not bounded yet (#139).
- **Nobody signs "none held",** where a CA signs "good" with a time: only the identity could, and where a working key signs it is kept away from daily work (§5), and a working key's word for its own certificate is the thief's word too. So it has no lifetime to hold, and costs one call per post; a server that falsely answers none is believed, as on the web.

**Not read:** how Signal, Matrix and Keybase handle a compromised identity or device.

**Where ours differs, and why:**
- **The default is Signal's, not email's:** a stranger's messages are held, because a mailbox's reader is often an agent that acts on what it reads, and a verified sender proves who wrote, not that the mailbox wanted it. Holding is no refusal, so the sender is told nothing, as in Signal.
- **A sender the ACL refuses is told** (`403 sender_not_allowed`, §11.10), as Matrix's `M_FORBIDDEN` and email's X.7.1, where Signal's block is silent: silence would need the server to take and drop the message, a receipt for a message never read, or the client to drop it, spending the mailbox's caps. A mailbox wanting silence leaves a sender unallowed.
- **Matrix's reading, with two changes:** no `allow` is refused, not read as nobody, so a writer that drops an empty list fails loudly; an entry is an address or `*`, since a pattern over part of a hash means nothing and would let in an address made to match, and an address has one lowercase form, so nothing is case-insensitive. A deny, as Matrix's, changes nothing stored (§11.14): the mailbox drains what it holds, and the deny refuses that sender's later sends.
- **Writers are kept apart by `If-Match` on the server's opaque `ETag`, `412` when false** (RFC 9110 §13.1.1), as Cloud Storage's precondition and Kubernetes' `resourceVersion`; a bare `PUT` replaces. Never a number a writer picks: one that must rise lets a writer take the highest, and a leaked pass could freeze the §11.14 pause for good.
- **A `PUT` answers no `ETag`,** as RFC 9110 §9.3.4 requires: the server sorts and dedupes entries, so what it holds may not be what was sent.
- **Too many entries is `413`,** where S3 answers its count limits `400`: the entries bound the record's size.
- **No `DELETE`:** none means `{"allow":["*"],"deny":[]}`, as an S3 bucket always has an ACL.
- **The ACL sheds load and protects no reader:** Matrix's binds every server in a room; dromas's binds only the untrusted server, which by ignoring it can do only what it could already: drop messages, or deliver messages the mailbox would hold. The client's hold is what a reader relies on.

**Where ours differs, and why:**
- **No `reason` beside the code:** AIP-193 needs one because every Google API shares one code list; ours is this protocol's own, so a cause gets a code, AEP-193's way, by AIP-193's test of one error.
- **The words stay on the wire and no client of ours shows them,** where the API standards stop at "do not parse": the reader is often a model and the server is in the threat model, so a client takes only typed, checked values from it and gives its own sentence, as the agent guidance asks. curl still shows the words.
- **`type` is `tag:dromas.dev,2026-10-26:errors/<code>`, resolving to nothing,** where RFC 9457 §4 says a type URI SHOULD resolve to documentation: no client dereferences it, §11.3 documents the codes, and a URL would tie every server's errors to one site for the protocol's life. Zalando's relative types differ per server; a `tag:` does not. Its date is a whole day after the domain's registration, since `2026-10` would read as before it.
- **A code counts only under its own `type`,** RFC 9457's identifier: a body that names its problem twice and disagrees names none.
- **An unknown code, or one under another status, is no code:** any text fits a code's grammar, so the client prints the status alone.

## 3e. Output: JSON lines, each with its type first

**Ours:** every command prints JSON lines, `type` first, the same from the script: what it did and met to stdout, refusals included; to stderr only its failure, a wrong command line, or waiting. No `--json`, no switch on a terminal; help is the one text. A line nests the envelope whole as `envelope`, so what the envelope gains in v1 (§14) never meets the line's own names. A line that does not carry the envelope may carry one of its checked values as a member of its own, as `held`'s `from` and `sent`'s `to` do: nothing is spread, so nothing meets (client#441 item 3).

# Part 3 — the reference server's own choices

## 1. A store write has three outcomes

**Ours** (dromas-server's `ARCHITECTURE.md` § The store): each backend write is applied, refused as the store stated, or unknown, which may have applied, read by one function; a count reserved for it is given back only on a stated refusal.

**The precedent** (read 2026-10-24; § Sources). Go's `database/sql/driver`: "ErrBadConn should NOT be returned if there's a possibility that the database server might have performed the operation." (`driver.go`, Go 1.27.3). The MongoDB Go driver labels a commit of unknown outcome `UnknownTransactionCommitResult`, apart from a failure (`x/mongo/driver/errors.go`, `x/mongo/driver/operation.go` at `v2.9.2`), and `WithTransaction` retries on it (`mongo/session.go`).

## 2. What the server counts, on a listener of its own

**Ours** (dromas-server's `ARCHITECTURE.md` § Metrics): what the server does and refuses is counted on a listener apart from the API, off unless set, so limits are tuned and attacks seen without logs, which carry no caller's address.

**The precedent** (read 2026-10-19). Prometheus: "instrument everything", and for a request-serving system, queries, errors and latency (`prometheus/docs`, `docs/practices/instrumentation.md` at `410443d27b`). Let's Encrypt's Boulder serves `client_golang` metrics with the Go and process collectors on a separate listener, none when unset (`cmd/shell.go`, `newStatsRegistry`, at `cf6406dd86`).
